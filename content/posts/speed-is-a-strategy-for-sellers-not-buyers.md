Every season the same pitch shows up. A good park at a good price, broker-reported numbers, and four words doing all the work: "priced to sell quickly."

Any operator who has been at this a while has seen those four words. They serve the seller's timeline, and a seller's timeline can be legitimate or manufactured. Either way, it is not yours.

## Whose strategy is it

Speed is a strategy. The question is whose.

A buyer wants speed for one reason: to stop paying diligence costs and start collecting revenue. A seller wants speed for a different reason. Every day you spend verifying is a day the reported numbers sit under examination.

When the seller sets the clock, the buyer inherits the seller's version of the numbers. That is the whole game. "Tie it up and figure it out later" is not a strategy. It is an agreement to underwrite the seller's story.

## What speed does to a claim

Talk to enough sellers and the claims start to rhyme: the financials do not show the real revenue, a meaningful share of it arrives in cash, and the real numbers live in a separate ledger.

Claims made on a call are easier to test when the questions were set before the call started, and the [27-Question Seller Call Script](/resources/27-question-seller-call-script) is that set, in order, while the clock is still the buyer's to use.

Some of that is true. Some of it is memory. Some of it is salesmanship. All of it is broker-reported until a bank statement, a tax return, or a deposit record says otherwise.

Here is what speed does to those claims: it turns them into underwriting inputs. A thirty-day close does not give you time to reconcile the cash story to anything. So the cash story goes into the model at face value, and you pay a verified price for an unverified number.

Sellers control the clock in both directions, and some deadlines are real: a loan maturity, a 1031 exchange, a partnership split. A 1031 exchange is the clearest example, because its deadlines are written down and do not move. Under the IRS guidance for like-kind exchanges under IRC Section 1031, the seller has 45 days from the sale of the relinquished property to identify potential replacement properties in writing, and the replacement property must be received and the exchange completed no later than 180 days after that sale, or by the due date of the tax return for that year, whichever is earlier. A seller working against that clock has a real deadline. The deadline still does not transfer to the buyer as a reason to skip verification. But when the seller who needs an answer by Friday takes three weeks to produce the rent roll, the urgency is manufactured on the way in and the evidence is rationed on the way out. Either way, a compressed window with slow evidence decides what gets verified before the wire, and it is not you deciding.

When the deadline is real, structure can sometimes do the work speed was supposed to do, and [Seller Note Anatomy](/resources/seller-note-anatomy) shows how what the seller fears becomes note terms they can say yes to.

A compressed diligence window is the mechanism. It is what lets "trust me" survive until the wire transfer.

There is a reason the language works. When money is cheap, owners tend to hold the good parks. In our judgment, strong parks often move through relationships and quiet processes before a broad public listing, though plenty still reach the open market. So the parks that do come to market arrive with a story attached, and the story usually includes a reason to hurry. Manufactured urgency keeps the buyer from asking the one question that matters: why is this one for sale?

## What fits inside a 30-day close, and what drops off

**Illustrative timeline (labeled illustrative, not a schedule for any specific deal):** a standard diligence sequence runs deposit and collections reconciliation, rent roll and lease sampling, utility bills and capacity documentation, zoning and nonconforming-use verification, survey and title review, insurance and property-tax confirmation, a site walk and unit sampling, and lender and appraisal steps. Compressed into thirty days, the work sorts roughly like this:

- **Fits, if records arrive promptly:** deposit and collections reconciliation for the trailing period, rent roll review with a lease sample, utility bill review, insurance and tax confirmation from the assessor and a broker, and a first site walk.
- **Fits only with luck and fast third parties:** utility capacity letters, a zoning verification letter, survey delivery, and lender appraisal scheduling. Each depends on someone outside the deal answering on the buyer's clock.
- **Typically drops off:** a full lease-by-lease audit, estoppel certificates across the park, capacity confirmation for any expansion claim, resolution of title or survey exceptions, and a second pass at any number that failed to reconcile the first time. These are usually the items that were carrying the open questions.

That sorting is the point. A thirty-day close does not delete the dropped items. It converts them into post-closing discoveries.

## Earnest money: when the deposit goes hard

Earnest money is where the clock becomes money, so its mechanics belong in the open, not in a footnote. The purchase agreement controls the exact terms, and terms vary by contract and jurisdiction, but the exposure points follow a common pattern.

- **At signing:** the buyer deposits earnest money with the escrow or title holder. During the diligence or feasibility period, the deposit is typically refundable if the buyer terminates in writing within that period and under the contract's terms. The buyer's real exposure in this window is the money already spent on inspections, reports, travel, and professionals, which no contract refunds.
- **At the diligence deadline:** this is the gate that matters. When the diligence period expires without termination, the deposit typically goes hard, meaning it becomes non-refundable except where the contract preserves a refund, commonly for a seller default or a title problem the seller cannot cure. From this gate forward, walking away usually costs the deposit.
- **At extensions and later gates:** extension options, financing deadlines, and additional deposits can each move more money from refundable to hard. Every gate should be read as a price: what amount becomes non-refundable, on what date, for what additional time or right.
- **At a buyer default after the deposit is hard:** the seller's remedy is often the deposit itself, kept as agreed damages under the contract, on top of the buyer's sunk diligence costs. That total, deposit plus sunk costs, is the buyer's actual exposure, and it should be calculated before the clock starts, not after.

Read the earnest-money clauses the way the seller's broker hopes you will not: slowly, gate by gate, with the dates on a calendar.

## Labeled speed is safe speed

This is not an argument against moving fast. The labels do not slow a deal down. They speed up the right parts of it.

Run the seller's clock through the six labels. The asking price and the pitch are broker-reported. The deposit records you actually receive are verified. The documents the seller has not produced are missing evidence, and they stay missing evidence no matter how loud the clock ticks. The revenue you modeled from the seller's story is an underwriting assumption. Label it before it enters the model, or the model will treat it as a fact.

*Editor's note (2026-10-05): this note was written when the public language used four evidence states. The [ParkProof Standard](/parkproof-standard) now uses six: Verified, Broker-reported, Missing evidence, Underwriting assumption, Calculated, and Unresolved.*

A fast close built on labeled evidence is a strategy. A fast close built on unlabeled claims is a donation.

A seller can genuinely believe the property produces more than the records show. Belief is not fraud. But belief does not underwrite. If a number cannot be reconciled to a reliable source, it cannot carry weight in the underwriting or the financing, no matter how sincere the person saying it.

These are not unusual stories. In our judgment, buyers who hurry on a seller's timeline often end up paying tuition on this lesson. The only question is whether you pay it during diligence or during ownership.

A deadline met on a labeled record is a decision. A deadline met on hope is a closing that reopens.

The IRS states the two time limits used above: 45 days from the sale of the relinquished property to identify potential replacement properties in writing, and completion of the exchange no later than 180 days after that sale or the due date, with extensions, of the tax return for the year of the sale, whichever is earlier. The IRS also states that these limits generally cannot be extended except in the case of presidentially declared disasters.

The timeline in this piece tests what that standard window can and cannot hold.
- **Limits:** the timeline above is a labeled illustration, not a schedule or a promise about any specific deal. Earnest-money terms, refund rights, and default remedies are controlled by the signed purchase agreement and by applicable law, and they vary by contract and jurisdiction. The pattern described here is the common structure to look for, not a substitute for reading the actual agreement, with counsel where the amounts warrant it.
